# Reading Mode prompt

Create a deep educational case study from a real event in business, finance, artificial intelligence, technology, or corporate strategy. This is not a news summary. Use the event as an entry point, then progressively abstract it into reusable knowledge.

If I provide a case, use it. If I do not, select a specific, well-documented case with a meaningful and transferable mechanism. Prefer recent cases when appropriate and historical cases when unusually instructive. Avoid repeating a familiar lesson merely because a headline is popular. Do not use private, employer-confidential, or personally identifying information.

When current research is available, verify material claims. Prefer filings, audited reports, earnings materials, official announcements, transaction documents, and regulator decisions, supplemented by high-quality financial reporting. Do not invent figures. Clearly distinguish Fact, Interpretation, and Assumption when that distinction matters.

Write for an intelligent reader who knows basic business concepts but may not know the company or industry. Introduce jargon in plain language. Let the reader discover concepts through the sequence specific example, mechanism, abstraction.

Use this shared analytical engine:

1. Establish the event: what happened, who was involved, what changed, and why it became financially or strategically interesting.
2. Follow the money: trace the relevant revenue, pricing, costs, margins, cash flow, capital allocation, unit economics, financing, balance-sheet effects, opportunity cost, and risk-reward asymmetry. Use only the concepts that explain the case.
3. Map stakeholders: for each important party, explain what it is optimizing for, what it knows, what incentives it faces, and where interests align or conflict.
4. Explain the incentive mechanism in plain language before naming it. Consider principal-agent problems, Goodhart's Law, moral hazard, adverse selection, metric gaming, coordination failure, short-term versus long-term incentives, lock-in, cannibalization, or silos only when relevant.
5. Explain the strategic mechanism as a causal chain. Use only relevant frameworks, which may include competitive advantage, switching costs, network effects, vertical integration, platform economics, bundling, pricing power, scale, scope, option value, real options, capital allocation, build-buy-partner choices, disruption, or path dependency.
6. Add the management and financial planning and analysis view when useful: metrics, leading and lagging indicators, a potentially dangerous key performance indicator, forecast assumptions, sensitivity analysis, and a decision-changing threshold.
7. Abstract the case. State that the case looks like it is about X, but structurally it is about Y. Derive a framework and recognition cues that travel to another industry.
8. Frame the decision. Steelman multiple sides, including doing nothing, alternative uses of capital, second-order effects, and the assumptions required for management's choice to work.
9. End with exactly one difficult discussion question. It must have no obvious answer, require reasoning from the case, contain a genuine trade-off, and put the reader in a decision-making role. Ask for a choice, the most sensitive assumption, and evidence that would reverse the choice. Put nothing after this question.

Optimize for visual comprehension. Use Markdown headings, bullets, tables, compact financial calculations, and comparison matrices when helpful. A strong structure is:

# Case title

## What happened

## Why this matters

## Follow the money

## Stakeholders and incentives

## The strategic mechanism

## FP&A / management view

## The deeper framework

## What could go wrong

## Transferable lesson

## Your decision

Be conversational and rigorous. Do not reduce the analysis to a listicle. Maintain analytical neutrality and do not automatically agree with management.
