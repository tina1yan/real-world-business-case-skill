# Listening Mode prompt

Create a deep educational case study from a real event in business, finance, artificial intelligence, technology, or corporate strategy. This is not a news summary. It should sound like a thoughtful mini business podcast for someone listening through text-to-speech while driving.

If I provide a case, use it. If I do not, select a specific, well-documented case with a meaningful and transferable mechanism. Prefer recent cases when appropriate and historical cases when unusually instructive. Avoid repeating a familiar lesson merely because a headline is popular. Do not use private, employer-confidential, or personally identifying information.

When current research is available, verify material claims. Prefer filings, audited reports, earnings materials, official announcements, transaction documents, and regulator decisions, supplemented by high-quality financial reporting. Do not invent figures. Clearly distinguish facts, interpretations, and assumptions in natural spoken language when that distinction matters.

Write for an intelligent listener who knows basic business concepts but may not know the company or industry. Explain jargon on first use. Let the listener discover concepts through a specific example, then the mechanism, then the abstraction.

Use the full analytical engine below. Audio-friendly does not mean simplified or shorter.

First, establish the event: what happened, who was involved, what changed, and why it became financially or strategically interesting.

Then follow the money. Trace the relevant revenue, pricing, costs, margins, cash flow, capital allocation, unit economics, financing, balance-sheet effects, opportunity cost, and risk-reward asymmetry. Select only the concepts that genuinely explain the case.

Next, map the important stakeholders. Explain what each is optimizing for, what information each possesses, what incentives each faces, and where interests align or conflict.

Then explain the incentive mechanism in plain language before naming it. Relevant mechanisms can include a principal-agent problem, Goodhart's Law, moral hazard, adverse selection, metric gaming, coordination failure, short-term versus long-term incentives, platform lock-in, cannibalization, or organizational silos.

Explain the strategic mechanism as a causal chain. Use only relevant frameworks, which may include competitive advantage, switching costs, network effects, vertical integration, platform economics, bundling, pricing power, scale, scope, option value, real options, capital allocation, build-buy-partner choices, disruption, or path dependency.

When it improves the case, take the management and financial planning and analysis view. Describe the most useful metrics, leading and lagging indicators, a key performance indicator that could accidentally cause bad behavior, the forecast assumptions that matter most, a sensitivity analysis, and the threshold at which the decision should change.

Then abstract the case. Say what it appears to be about and what it is structurally about. Derive a framework and recognition cues that transfer to another company or industry.

Finally, frame the decision. Present the strongest credible arguments on multiple sides, including doing nothing, alternative uses of capital, second-order effects, reversibility, and the assumptions management needs to be right.

End with exactly one difficult discussion question. It must have no obvious answer, require reasoning from the case, contain a real trade-off, and put the listener in the role of a chief executive officer, chief financial officer, financial planning and analysis leader, investor, regulator, product leader, or another decision maker. Ask for a choice, the assumption that matters most, and the evidence that would reverse the choice. Put nothing after the question.

Follow these audio rules:

- Do not use Markdown blockquotes.
- Avoid quotation marks and long direct quotations. Prefer paraphrasing.
- Do not use visually dependent tables.
- Turn comparisons into spoken comparisons and equations into natural spoken language.
- Avoid symbols and markup that text-to-speech may vocalize awkwardly.
- Expand uncommon abbreviations the first time they appear.
- Express large numbers naturally.
- Minimize parentheses and dense bullet sequences in the produced case.
- Use natural spoken transitions so a listener who never sees the screen always knows where the analysis is and how it connects.

Useful transitions include: First, let us look at what actually happened. Now the interesting part is the money. Next, let us look at the incentives. Here is where the case becomes more general. Now imagine you are the chief financial officer. The deeper lesson is this. Finally, here is the decision I want you to make.

Maintain analytical neutrality. Do not automatically praise or criticize management. Preserve the same financial and strategic depth that a strong Reading Mode case would contain.
